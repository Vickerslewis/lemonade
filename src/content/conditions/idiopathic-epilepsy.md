## Summary

Idiopathic epilepsy was confirmed after a brain MRI in May 2026 and re-confirmed by Northwest Veterinary Specialists on 26 October 2026. No structural brain abnormality, tumour or metabolic cause has been found. Lemonade has had five seizures in about 11 months. The two most recent formed a cluster (two within 24 hours) on 22 October 2026, and because of that cluster the neurologist now recommends treatment. He is unusual in having clear, reliable warning signs (a prodrome) 18–24 hours before a seizure.

## Diagnostic findings

- **Brain MRI (May 2026):** no tumours; structurally normal brain; no hydrocephalus or other concerning findings
- A mild increase of spinal fluid (CSF) around the midbrain/brainstem — probably congenital; causes no clinical problems; in the wrong location to explain seizures (re-reviewed 26 October 2026)
- **Bloods:** normal after the first seizure (November 2025) and on a small panel at the emergency vet (22 October 2026). Baseline haematology and biochemistry taken 26 October 2026 — results {{TBD}}
- **Conclusion:** structural and metabolic causes excluded; idiopathic epilepsy criteria met. See [imaging](/imaging/)

## Clinical signs

Seizure characteristics (5 recorded events — see the [seizure log](/seizures/)):

- All five generalised tonic-clonic (whole body)
- Duration 50 seconds to ~1 minute 20 seconds
- All during sleep or at night
- Onset: while asleep he slowly lifts his head at an odd angle, looks up at the ceiling, then seizes
- Rarely urinates; never defecates; mild salivation afterwards
- Seizures 4 and 5 were a cluster, 5 h 47 min apart

Warning signs (prodrome):

- About 18–24 hours before, usually at night: extremely nervous, wakes people up, won't settle for 3–4 hours, stares at the ceiling, pants, noise-sensitive (especially wind)
- Seen before seizures 1, 2 and 4
- Several warning-sign nights in 2026 were not followed by a seizure (low-stimulus environment kept for 24 hours)

After a seizure (post-ictal):

- Seizures 1 and 2: aggressive barking and biting; did not recognise his parents; resolved within ~20–40 minutes
- Seizure 3: calm on waking; no aggression
- Seizure 4: barking; settled in ~15 minutes
- Seizure 5: lay on his side without moving for ~30–45 seconds before getting up; erratic, then calm within 10–15 minutes

## Management

### Medication

- **Plan (chosen 26 October 2026):** no daily medication. Levetiracetam 1,000 mg (1 tablet) every 8 hours for 5 days whenever the warning signs are recognised. Follow the [action plan](/action-plan/)
- Post-cluster levetiracetam course (22–26 October 2026) finished — see [medications](/medications/)
- **Next step if needed:** daily phenobarbital with regular blood monitoring
- **Rescue:** rectal diazepam as soon as a seizure reaches 3 minutes

### Diet and supplements

- MCT oil daily since the first seizure; the neurologist endorsed it — see [diet and supplements](/care/diet-and-supplements/)
- No evidence for avoiding specific proteins or gluten

### Monitoring

- Record every warning-sign episode and every seizure, including when levetiracetam was started
- Contact Northwest after any seizure
- Recheck at Northwest within 6 months to stay under specialist care

### Environment during and after a seizure

- Keep it quiet; dim or turn off lights; close windows to reduce outside noise
- Don't restrain him — observe from a safe distance
- Supervise walks until he has fully recovered

> [!important]
> **Seek emergency vet care if** a seizure lasts longer than 3 minutes (give rectal diazepam at 3 minutes, then go), two or more seizures happen within 24 hours, he doesn't recover normally between seizures, or new neurological signs (weakness, blindness, behaviour change) persist.

## Prognosis

Idiopathic epilepsy is managed, not cured; the aim is fewer seizures rather than none. About 70% of dogs respond to medication. Some become drug-resistant and need several drugs. Lemonade's starting point (five seizures in ~11 months) is good, and the neurologist saw nothing to suggest he will be drug-resistant. It is likely genetic. A very small number of epileptic dogs die suddenly for unknown reasons (SUDEP); this is rare.
