## Treatment discussion

The clinician recommended treatment now, mainly because of the cluster. Clusters raise the risk of status epilepticus, and repeated seizures may make further seizures more likely.

| Option | Clinician's view |
|---|---|
| Imepitoin (Pexion) | Not recommended. Good for occasional single seizures, not for dogs that cluster |
| Potassium bromide | Not first-line; more side effects. Reserved for later if needed |
| Zonisamide | Slightly less potent than phenobarbital; used mainly as an add-on in the UK |
| **Option 1 — Phenobarbital (daily, ongoing)** | Best evidence after a cluster; reduces seizure frequency in ~70% of dogs |
| **Option 2 — Levetiracetam when the warning signs start** | Less evidence, but reasonable for Lemonade because his warning signs are so clear and reliable |

### Option 1 — Phenobarbital

- Aim is fewer seizures, not none; elimination is uncommon
- Early side effects: sedation and wobbliness in all four legs, worst at the start; usually kept on the same dose until levels settle at ~2 weeks
- Monitoring: blood count, biochemistry and serum phenobarbital level 2 weeks after starting, then every 3–6 months. Target level 25–30
- Affects the liver to some degree: ALT/ALP often rise slightly, which is acceptable if liver function is fine. Severe liver effects are uncommon (the clinician has stopped it in ~3–4 dogs)
- Works by raising the seizure threshold (boosting inhibitory signalling in the brain)
- Can be stopped, but must be weaned over ~6 weeks, never stopped abruptly

### Option 2 — Prodrome-triggered levetiracetam

- When the warning signs are recognised, give levetiracetam for **5 days**: **1 tablet (1,000 mg) every 8 hours**, about 30 mg/kg
- Goal: stop the seizure, or at least stop a cluster
- Benefits: not on medication all the time, no ongoing liver monitoring, very well tolerated (occasional sleepiness)
- Risks: may only delay the need for phenobarbital; a seizure or cluster could still happen
- Giving it after a "false alarm" is fine; no meaningful downside
- Clinician's personal view: they would choose this for their own dog, and it suits Lemonade because his records are detailed and his parents work from home and can act quickly
- Success is judged over months, not one event: one seizure in four months would not necessarily mean switching to phenobarbital. Keep counting warning-sign episodes and seizures

## Other advice

- **Rescue diazepam:** give it if a seizure passes **3 minutes**, to avoid reaching 5 minutes (status epilepticus). Brain damage becomes a risk after ~10 minutes. It can be repeated if there is no response within 1–2 minutes
- **During a seizure:** move anything that could hurt him only if it is safe to; put something soft under his head on a hard floor if safe. Keep hands away from his mouth, because jaw chattering causes bites
- **No evidence for:** cooling towels on paws (only matters in dangerously long seizures), cutting out pork, beef or gluten, or diet trials for triggers. Most dogs have no identifiable trigger
- **Ice cream after a seizure:** harmless as a treat, but it only helps seizures caused by low blood sugar, which is not his problem
- **SUDEP:** a very small number of epileptic dogs die suddenly for unknown reasons. Mentioned for awareness; the clinician has never seen it in a patient
- **Genetics:** likely genetic; it cannot be predicted which offspring would be affected
- **Outlook:** ~70% of dogs respond to medication. Some become drug-resistant and need several drugs (vagal nerve stimulator as a last resort). Nothing about Lemonade so far suggests he will be one of them
- **Lifestyle:** don't build life around the epilepsy. Travel abroad is possible with a plan agreed with Northwest in advance
