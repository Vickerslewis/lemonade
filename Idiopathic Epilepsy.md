# Idiopathic Epilepsy

> Confirmed idiopathic epilepsy following brain MRI in May 2026; no structural cause identified. Three seizures to date; anti-epileptic medication not yet started.

## Summary

Confirmed idiopathic epilepsy following brain MRI (2026-05). No structural brain abnormality, tumour, or other identifiable cause found. This is the most common and most treatable form of canine epilepsy. Three seizures recorded to date; anti-epileptic medication has not been started.

## Clinical Signs

Seizure characteristics (based on 3 recorded events — see [[Seizure Log]] for full detail):

- All three seizures were generalised (whole body)
- Duration 50 seconds to ~1 minute 20 seconds
- All three occurred during sleep or at night

Known pre-ictal pattern:
- Episodes 1 and 2 preceded by ~24 hours of heightened anxiety, restlessness, pacing, staring, and sensitivity to sounds (especially wind and household noises)
- Pre-ictal behaviour for episode 3 not documented

Post-ictal pattern:
- Episodes 1 and 2: aggressive barking and biting; did not recognise owner; resolved within ~20–30 minutes
- Episode 3: calm on waking; no aggression

## Management

Anti-epileptic medication has not been started. The decision to begin medication is typically guided by seizure frequency, cluster events, or prolonged seizures. Continue monitoring via [[Seizure Log]].

Environmental management during and after a seizure:
- Keep the environment quiet
- Dim or turn off lights
- Close windows to reduce external noise
- Do not restrain — observe from a safe distance
- Supervise walks until fully recovered

> [!warning]
> **Seek emergency vet care immediately if:**
> - Any single seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes
> - Two or more seizures occur within 24 hours
> - He does not recover normally between seizures
> - New neurological signs (weakness, blindness, behaviour change) persist

## Prognosis

Idiopathic epilepsy is manageable in most dogs. With appropriate monitoring and, when needed, anti-epileptic medication, many dogs maintain a good quality of life. Seizure frequency and severity should be tracked over time to guide treatment decisions.
